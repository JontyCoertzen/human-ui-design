# Logo design: research, sketch, combine, reduce

This is a **repeatable design process**, not a logo generator or a library of motifs. Never impose a favourite typeface, hand, heart, monogram, colour, shape, or metaphor on a new brand. An icon should be specific to its owner, capable of standing alone, and usable in the media the client actually needs.

## Route by task

- **New identity:** Complete discovery and divergence before proposing polished marks.
- **Exploration requested:** Generate a broad, numbered study with genuinely different concepts. Do not stop at one polished image.
- **Existing logo refinement:** Preserve the approved concept; change the requested component and test it against the source.
- **Technical production:** Inspect the actual vector file and printer's specifications. A generated preview is not production artwork.
- **Audit:** Critique first, with concrete visual evidence; do not edit without authorization.

## 1. Discover the idea worth representing

Read the provided sketch, history, mission, existing identity, website, packaging, and briefs **before asking questions**. Use [the logo brief](../assets/LOGO_BRIEF.template.md), leaving unknowns explicitly unknown. Establish:

- **Audience and purpose:** Who sees the mark, what should it feel like, and what does the organization actually do?
- **Meaning:** The human truth, relationship, promise or behaviour behind the brand. Distinguish this from a literal object the icon must depict.
- **Visual inheritance:** What an old sketch or logo *means* versus details that made it too complicated. Preserve significance, not necessarily its drawing style.
- **Applications:** Website/header, social avatar, favicons, print, signage, signage viewed from distance, apparel, screen print, heat press, embroidery, engraving, vinyl cut. Ask for actual minimum sizes and production methods if essential.
- **Constraints:** Approved colours/type, symbol-only and wordmark needs, single-colour requirement, cultural context, accessibility, licensing, mark ownership and approvals.
- **Landscape:** Research relevant existing identities, direct competitors and common industry visual clichés. Record the sources and differences. Learn principles from references; **do not copy** their contours or distinctive composition.

Write a one-sentence design problem and three words describing the intended *feeling*, not a preselected visual style. Do not force every part of the organization’s story into one icon. A strong mark may express one central relationship while the broader identity tells the rest.

## 2. Diverge before polishing

Start in **solid black on white**. Colour can disguise a weak silhouette. Draw rapidly, without a wordmark or mockup for every idea.

1. Identify 3–6 genuinely different creative territories: e.g. gesture, relationship, letterform, negative space, typography-led, material/object, or locally meaningful reference. These are possible methods, **not mandatory visual motifs**.
2. Produce a broad numbered thumbnail set, normally **24–50 quick marks** for open logo exploration. In an extended exploration, exceed 50 if the ideas remain repetitive. Respect a user's requested minimum; minor approved-logo edits do not require a fresh 50.
3. Make at least two distinct exploration passes: one broad and one that deliberately challenges the first pass. The first 10–15 ideas often expose the expected clichés; do not mistake that first batch for a final shortlist.
4. Label every sketch with an immutable ID (`S01`, `S02`, …); show contact sheets so the user can cite parts. Keep explorations and rejected directions visible during the decision, not just the latest rendered favourite.
5. Vary **structure**, not just embellishments: silhouette, orientation, shape grammar, negative space, visual metaphor, number of masses, rhythm, and relationship to lettering. Avoid 16 copies of one stock symbol with a changed central dot.

Image generation can accelerate **concept previews** and rough contact sheets. Use it for multiple exploratory variants, not as proof that geometry is original, printable or vector. Check the output actually has the requested count and that IDs are legible; if not, regenerate or assemble a precise numbered sheet in a vector design tool. A user-provided sketch is a **reference** unless they explicitly request an edit of that image.

## 3. Build and mix components intentionally

When a promising concept depends on several pieces, decompose it into a library before combining:

| Layer | Questions |
| --- | --- |
| Base silhouette | What makes the mark identifiable at a glance? What can be removed? |
| Inner form | Is a person/object/letter needed, or does open space say enough? |
| Relationship | Is it lifting, enclosing, connecting, sheltering, revealing, reaching? |
| Negative space | Can one cutout do the work of an extra drawing? |
| Wordmark | Must the symbol carry the name, or is the name a separate lockup? |

Create 3–6 **base forms** and 3–5 **counterforms or relationship treatments** where the brief warrants it. Cross them in a labeled matrix (`A1`–`D4`), keeping row/column components recognizable so elements can actually be swapped. Also make a second, less constrained sketch sheet to avoid optimizing one generic formula.

For example, a mark may need one recognizable gesture **or** one abstract expression of support; adding four literal people, a landscape, a heart and an initial is not automatically more meaningful. If a component reads as an unintended leaf, bird, boat, medical sign or stock icon, record that failure and redraw the underlying silhouette instead of adding more explanation.

## 4. Critique and shortlist without confirmation bias

Review contact sheets at thumbnail size before reading the rationales. Use these questions:

1. **Recognition:** Can it be identified or recalled after a brief glance? Does the critical idea survive without the name?
2. **Specificity:** Could a competitor use the same mark unchanged? Does it resemble stock iconography?
3. **Restraint:** Is every component doing necessary work? Would deleting one improve it?
4. **Silhouette:** Is it recognizable in solid black and reversed white? Are gaps and counters robust?
5. **Unintended readings:** Does it become another object, a cultural symbol, or something awkward at small sizes?
6. **Use:** Does it work with the approved brand and required manufacturing constraints?

Record strengths **and failures** by sketch ID. Choose about **five distinct ideas**, not the five prettiest versions of the same idea. Roughly refine those five, then present about **three defensible finalists** when ready. Do not claim numeric scores from Awwwards, Taste or an automated logo checker unless those actual checks took place. A subjective internal rubric is not certification.

If feedback is “too literal,” remove depicted objects and work with relationships/negative space. If “too abstract,” restore one recognizable anchor. If “too generic,” revisit the **creative premise and form family**, not just colours, fonts or corners. Do not reflexively choose the first option the agent previously praised.

## 5. Refine a selected mark in vector

- Reconstruct winning geometry as **actual editable vector paths** (SVG, AI, EPS or equivalent), not an auto-labelled raster inside an SVG wrapper. Check path joins, accidental holes, smoothness and optical balance.
- Adjust stroke/shape weight, counterspaces and optical alignment at intended sizes. Purely mathematical centering can look off.
- Pair the symbol with a considered wordmark; customize kerning and lockup if needed. Reuse approved typography for an existing identity unless the user authorizes changing it.
- Develop **symbol-only**, horizontal and stacked lockups; full-colour, single-black, single-white/reversed, and two-colour versions only as appropriate. Never require two inks for recognition.
- Keep colours tied to brand reasons and print processes. Do not apply a palette to rough sketches merely to make them look more finished.

## 6. Prove it in the real world

Build a proof sheet at the **actual physical sizes** requested: small embroidery/cap, screen print, heat transfer, letterhead, social avatar, favicon, large signage. An AIGA teaching exercise uses a **½-inch (12.7 mm)** black-and-white logo test as a useful stress case; it is not a universal minimum size.

Check with the actual vendor. For example, [Printful’s embroidery guide](https://help.printful.com/hc/en-us/articles/50263985175953-How-should-I-prepare-my-design-for-embroidery) currently specifies for its flat embroidery process minimum 0.05-inch (~1.27 mm) line thickness and 0.25-inch (~6.35 mm) negative-space gaps. These are **vendor/process-specific**, not universal rules. At very small sizes, create a reduced-detail embroidery variant instead of pretending thin gaps will survive. Ask for a stitched/printed sample when practical.

Do not claim a file is ready for merchandise based only on AI-generated preview images. Check:

- Black, white/reversed, and spot-colour separations; contrast on light/dark fabric.
- Thick enough solids/gaps, no unintended islands, fragile lines or embroidery fill problems.
- True vector paths and correct export/artwork dimensions, with transparent backgrounds where required.
- Readability without wordmark at its smallest intended use, and legibility of the wordmark where included.
- Similarity and potential trademark conflicts; research relevant registers before commercial rollout and recommend professional clearance where risk warrants it. Do not certify legal uniqueness.

## 7. Handoff and continuity

Deliver the **sketch sheets**, labeled component library, selected and rejected IDs with reasoning, and proof sheet. Once approved, provide editable source art, exports and minimal identity usage rules: variants, colours, clear space, minimum size, placement, and forbidden modifications. Record decisions in the project's `DESIGN.md` or brand manual so future work follows the approved identity.

Be explicit about what was **actually verified** (e.g. one-colour screenshot, real SVG inspection, vendor sample) versus what remains a prototype. If the user is still exploring, **do not publish a final logo or update a live site** without that authorization.

## Research foundations

- [AIGA — Generate Ideas](https://www.aiga.org/sites/default/files/2021-03/3D_DesignProcess_GenerateIdeas.pdf): breadth of thumbnail exploration; 50-sketch exercise; early clichés.
- [AIGA — Design Development](https://www.aiga.org/sites/default/files/2021-03/3E_DesignProcess_DesignDevelopment.pdf): select five ideas, refine three; black-and-white and small-size proofs.
- [Pentagram — Gato](https://www.pentagram.com/news/gato): multiple sketches drawn from project-specific context before a simpler legible winner.
- [Pentagram — Broadway Housing Communities](https://www.pentagram.com/work/broadway-housing-communities): a nonprofit identity differentiating itself from predictable charity iconography.
- [Pentagram — DK](https://www.pentagram.com/work/dk): iterative simplification to a flexible one-colour mark.
- [Pentagram — Mozilla Foundation](https://www.pentagram.com/work/mozilla-foundation): systematic extraction and recombination of distinctive geometric building blocks.
- [Printful — Embroidery preparation](https://help.printful.com/hc/en-us/articles/50263985175953-How-should-I-prepare-my-design-for-embroidery): production-specific limits and warning that generated designs require real checks.
